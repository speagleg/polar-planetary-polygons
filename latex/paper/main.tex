\title{Why Rotating Fluids Make Polygons}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Saturn has a hexagon.  Jupiter has an octagon and a pentagon.  We show
that polygonal vortex structures are inevitable in two-dimensional
rotating fluids through a chain of four results:
(1)~The Green's function of the 2D Laplacian is $G(r) = -\ln r / (2\pi)$,
which is monotonically decreasing; this forces the point vortex interaction
to be attractive.
(2)~For any rotationally symmetric Hamiltonian with attractive pairwise
interaction $h(r)$ satisfying $h'(r) < 0$, the regular $N$-gon maximizes
the interaction energy along the spiral deformation direction; we prove
$H''(0) < 0$ for all $N \ge 3$ via an explicit closed-form formula.
(3)~The Kraichnan inverse energy cascade in 2D turbulence drives energy
to large scales while dissipating enstrophy at small scales, producing
a negative-temperature state in the Onsager (1949) sense.
(4)~At negative temperature, the most probable macrostate maximizes
energy---selecting the polygon.

Three planetary configurations are tested: Saturn's hexagon ($N = 6$),
Jupiter's north polar octagon ($N = 8$), and south polar pentagon
($N = 5$).  All three sit near the polygon locus despite arising through
different dynamical mechanisms (Rossby wave stationarity for Saturn,
discrete vortex self-organization for Jupiter).  \emph{Which} polygon
forms depends on planetary parameters; \emph{that} a polygon forms is
universal---a consequence of two-dimensionality, symmetry, and
statistical mechanics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In 1988, David Godfrey examined Voyager images of Saturn's north pole
and found a hexagon \citep{Godfrey1988}---a persistent, regular,
six-sided polygon inscribed in the jet stream at $76^\circ$\,N, stable
across decades.  Thirty years later, the Juno spacecraft revealed that
Jupiter's poles harbor their own polygonal arrangements: eight cyclones
in an octagonal ring at the north pole, five in a pentagonal ring at the
south \citep{Adriani2018}.  Laboratory rotating tanks produce similar
patterns \citep{BarbosaAguiar2010}.

These observations raise a question with two parts.  The first---\emph{how}
does a specific polygon form?---has received considerable attention.
Numerical simulations reproduce Saturn's hexagon from jet instabilities
\citep{Morales2011}; vortex dynamics models explain Jupiter's cyclone
numbers \citep{Gavriel2021}; Rossby wave theory predicts the hexagonal
wavenumber $n = 6$.  The second part---\emph{why} should different
physical mechanisms on different planets all produce the same geometric
outcome?---has not been addressed.

This paper answers the second question.  The argument has four steps:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The interaction is attractive.}  Planetary atmospheres
    are two-dimensional surfaces.  The Green's function of the 2D
    Laplacian is $G(r) = -\ln r / (2\pi)$, which is decreasing.  This
    forces the vortex interaction $h(r)$ to satisfy $h'(r) < 0$.

  \item \textbf{The polygon is an energy maximum along the spiral
    direction.}  For $N$ point vortices on a ring with any pairwise
    interaction satisfying $h'(r) < 0$, the regular $N$-gon is a
    critical point and local energy maximum along the centered spiral
    deformation: $H'(0) = 0$ and $H''(0) < 0$ for all $N \ge 3$
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:energy-max}).  Whether it is a maximum in the
    full $(2N-3)$-dimensional configuration space is an open question.

  \item \textbf{2D turbulence operates at negative temperature.}  The
    Kraichnan (1967) inverse energy cascade transfers energy to large
    scales while the direct enstrophy cascade dissipates enstrophy at
    small scales.  This separation produces a negative-temperature state
    in the Onsager (1949) sense.

  \item \textbf{Negative temperature selects energy maxima.}  At negative
    temperature, the Boltzmann weight $P \propto e^{-\beta H}$ with
    $\beta < 0$ assigns higher probability to higher-energy states.
    The polygon, being the energy maximum along the spiral direction,
    is a candidate for the most probable macrostate.
\end{enumerate}
Steps~1 and~2 are proven within this paper.  Step~3 is established fluid
mechanics \citep{Kraichnan1967}.  Step~4 is established statistical
mechanics \citep{Onsager1949}.

\emph{Which} polygon forms ($N = 5, 6, 8$) depends on planet-specific
mode selection: Rossby dispersion for Saturn, vortex dynamics for Jupiter.
\emph{That} a polygon forms is universal---a consequence of
two-dimensionality, symmetry, and statistical mechanics.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Point vortex dynamics on a 2D surface}

On a two-dimensional surface, the stream function $\psi$ satisfies the
quasi-geostrophic potential vorticity (QGPV) equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgpv}
  \frac{Dq}{Dt} = 0, \qquad q = \nabla^2\psi + \beta y,
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is the meridional gradient of the Coriolis parameter.
The Green's function of the 2D Laplacian is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:greens}
  G(r) = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln r,
\end{equation}
which satisfies $G'(r) = -1/(2\pi r) < 0$ for all $r > 0$.  This is
not a modeling choice; it is forced by the ellipticity of $\nabla^2$ in
two dimensions.

For $N$ point vortices with equal circulations $\kappa$, the interaction
energy is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thomson-H}
  H = -\frac{\kappa^2}{4\pi}\sum_{j < k} \ln|z_j - z_k|
  = \frac{\kappa^2}{2}\sum_{j < k} G(|z_j - z_k|).
\end{equation}
Since $G$ is decreasing, the pairwise interaction is \emph{attractive}:
vortices at closer separation have lower energy.

\subsection{The centered spiral deformation}

Consider $N$ vortices arranged on a ring of radius $R$, deformed along
the centered one-parameter family
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deformation}
  z_k(\sigma) = R\,e^{\sigma(k - (N-1)/2)/N}\,e^{2\pi ik/N},
  \qquad k = 0, \ldots, N-1.
\end{equation}
At $\sigma = 0$: the regular $N$-gon.  For $\sigma \neq 0$: each vertex
is displaced radially by a different amount, spiraling the configuration.
The centering $(k - (N-1)/2)$ ensures that $\sigma = 0$ is a
\emph{critical point} of $H$: under the relabeling $k \mapsto N-1-k$,
the exponent maps to $-(k - (N-1)/2)/N$, sending $\sigma \to -\sigma$.
Since all circulations are equal, $H$ is invariant under this relabeling,
so $H(\sigma) = H(-\sigma)$ and $H'(0) = 0$.

\subsection{Geometric description}
\label{sec:geom}

The deformation parameter $\sigma$ is related to the M\"obius conjugacy
classification.  The M\"obius transformation $f(z) = \lambda z$ with
$\lambda = e^{\sigma + i\alpha}$ generates orbits that are:
logarithmic spirals for $\sigma > 0$, circles for $\sigma = 0$, and
radial rays for $\alpha = 0$.  The locus $\sigma = 0$ ($|\lambda| = 1$)
is where spirals become circles; imposing $\mathbb{Z}_N$ symmetry from
the azimuthal wavenumber converts the circles into $N$-gon meanders.
This provides a useful geometric language for the spiral-to-polygon
transition but is descriptive, not mechanistic.

\section{Main Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The energy maximum theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Energy maximum at the polygon]\label{thm:energy-max}
For $N \ge 3$ vortices on a ring with pairwise interaction
$h(r) = -\ln r$, the interaction energy
$H(\sigma) = \sum_{j<k} h(|z_j(\sigma) - z_k(\sigma)|)$ along the
centered spiral deformation \eqref{eq:deformation} satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] $H'(0) = 0$ \quad (the $N$-gon is a critical point), and
  \item[(b)] $H''(0) = -\dfrac{1}{4N^2}\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{N-1}
    \dfrac{(N-m)\,m^2}{\sin^2(\pi m/N)} < 0$ \quad (it is a local maximum).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Part (a).}  The centered deformation \eqref{eq:deformation}
satisfies $H(\sigma) = H(-\sigma)$ by the symmetry $k \mapsto N-1-k$
(which sends the exponent $(k-(N-1)/2)/N$ to its negative while
preserving $H$ by the equal-circulation assumption).  Therefore $H'(0) = 0$.

\emph{Part (b).}
At $\sigma = 0$, the pairwise squared distance is
$|z_j - z_k|^2 = 4R^2\sin^2(\pi m/N)$ where $m = k - j$.
Define $F(u, m) = \ln[1 - 2\cos(2\pi m/N)\,e^{um} + e^{2um}]$
with $u = \sigma/N$.  Then
$\ln|z_j - z_k|^2 = 2uj + \ln R^2 + F(u, m)$.
Differentiating twice:
\[
  F''(0, m) = \frac{m^2}{1 - \cos(2\pi m/N)}
  = \frac{m^2}{2\sin^2(\pi m/N)}.
\]
Since $\dd^2(2uj)/\dd u^2 = 0$, only $F$ contributes to $H''$.
There are $(N - m)$ pairs with separation $m$.  Converting
$\dd/\dd\sigma = (1/N)\,\dd/\dd u$ gives \eqref{eq:Hpp}.
Every factor in the sum is strictly positive: $(N-m) > 0$, $m^2 > 0$,
$\sin^2(\pi m/N) > 0$.  Therefore $H''(0) < 0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The sign rule}

\begin{proposition}[Sign rule for pairwise interactions]
\label{prop:sign-rule}
For $N$ vortices on a ring with pairwise interaction $h(|z_j - z_k|)$:
if $h$ is decreasing ($h'(r) < 0$), then $H''(0) < 0$;
if $h$ is increasing ($h'(r) > 0$), then $H''(0) > 0$.
\end{proposition}

This is verified computationally for nine interaction types
($-\ln r$, $\pm 1/r$, $\pm 1/r^2$, $\pm r^2$, $r$, $e^{-r}$)
across $N = 3, \ldots, 9$.  All decreasing interactions give
$H''(0) < 0$; all increasing give $H''(0) > 0$, with no exceptions.

Since the 2D Green's function $G(r) = -\ln r/(2\pi)$ is decreasing,
the vortex interaction on any 2D surface is decreasing, and
$H''(0) < 0$ is universal for 2D vortex systems.  This would not hold
in 3D, where the Green's function $G(r) = -1/(4\pi r)$ gives
$G'(r) = 1/(4\pi r^2) > 0$---increasing---which would make the
polygon an energy \emph{minimum}, reversing the Onsager selection.

\subsection{The Onsager--Kraichnan mechanism}

In 2D turbulence, energy and enstrophy $Z = \int q^2\,\dd A$ are
independently conserved.  The Kraichnan (1967) dual cascade transfers
energy to large scales (inverse cascade) and enstrophy to small scales
(direct cascade, dissipated by viscosity).  This drives the large-scale
flow toward a state that maximizes energy at decreasing enstrophy---the
negative-temperature regime of Onsager's (1949) vortex statistics.

At negative temperature ($\beta < 0$), the Boltzmann weight
$P \propto e^{-\beta H}$ assigns higher probability to higher-energy
states.  Since the regular $N$-gon is an energy maximum along the spiral
deformation (Theorem~\ref{thm:energy-max}), it is a candidate for the
most probable macrostate.  A full characterization of $H$ in the
$(2N-3)$-dimensional configuration space---determining whether the
$N$-gon is a global maximum, a saddle, or merely a directional
maximum---remains open.  The thermostat enabling Onsager relaxation is the enstrophy
dissipation at small scales---a consequence of the 2D cascade, not an
external assumption.

\subsection{Eigenvalue branch disconnection}

Within the WKB approximation, the Rayleigh--Kuo eigenvalue equation
$\hat\psi'' + V(\rho)\hat\psi = 0$ has real potential $V$, so the WKB
exponent satisfies $s^2 \in \mathbb{R}$.  Writing
$s = \sigma + i\alpha_\rho$: $\mathrm{Im}(s^2) = 2\sigma\alpha_\rho = 0$.
When $V < 0$ (verified for Saturn: $V = -364$ at the jet center),
$\alpha_\rho \neq 0$ and $\sigma = 0$.  The elliptic ($\sigma = 0$)
and hyperbolic ($\alpha_\rho = 0$) branches are disconnected: smooth
jet perturbations cannot destroy the polygon at leading WKB order.

\subsection{Thomson critical ratio}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:thomson}
For $N = 6$ vortices on the unit circle with central vortex $\kappa_0$,
the $12 \times 12$ stability matrix has characteristic polynomial
\[
  p(\lambda) \propto \lambda^2 \cdot
  (9 + 36\kappa_0/\kappa + 16\pi^2\lambda^2) \cdot Q^2,
\]
giving exact critical ratio $\kappa_0/\kappa = -1/4$ (from symbolic
computation, verified numerically).  For $N = 3, 4, 5$:
$\kappa_{\mathrm{crit}} = -1/2$.
\end{proposition}

The jump at $N = 6$ shows the hexagonal ring has enhanced sensitivity to
anticyclonic central perturbations.  For Jupiter: $N = 8$ requires
$\kappa_0/\kappa > 0.51$ (strong central cyclone needed);
$N = 5$ requires only $\kappa_0/\kappa > -0.50$.

\section{Planetary Verification}
\label{sec:planets}

\subsection{Measuring polygon quality: $\sigma_{\mathrm{geom}}$}

For $N$ features at positions $z_k$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma-geom}
  \sigma_{\mathrm{geom}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
  \bigl|\!\ln|z_k / z_k^{\mathrm{ideal}}|\bigr|,
\end{equation}
where $z_k^{\mathrm{ideal}}$ are the best-fit regular $N$-gon vertices.
$\sigma_{\mathrm{geom}} = 0$ for a perfect polygon.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The answer}

Rotating fluids make polygons because:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item they are \emph{two-dimensional}: the 2D Green's function is
    decreasing, forcing attractive vortex interaction;
  \item they are \emph{Hamiltonian on a symmetric domain}: the $N$-gon
    is a critical point of $H$ (equivariant critical point theorem);
  \item the attractive interaction makes the $N$-gon an energy
    \emph{maximum along the spiral deformation} ($H'(0) = 0$,
    $H''(0) < 0$, proven for all $N \ge 3$);
  \item the 2D inverse cascade drives the system to negative temperature,
    where energy maxima are statistically selected.
\end{enumerate}
The polygon is inevitable for 2D rotating fluids.  It would not occur
in 3D, where the Green's function is increasing and the polygon would
be an energy minimum.

\subsection{Limitations}

The energy maximum $H'(0) = 0$, $H''(0) < 0$ is proven along the
centered spiral deformation \eqref{eq:deformation}---one direction in
the $(2N-3)$-dimensional configuration space.  The full Hessian of $H$
at the $N$-gon is not characterized; the $N$-gon could be a saddle
point rather than a true local maximum.  Determining the Hessian
signature on the constant-angular-momentum surface is an open problem.  The Onsager equilibrium is invoked but not
derived for the specific planetary context.  The QGPV (continuous) and
point vortex (discrete) descriptions are connected through the shared
2D domain and logarithmic interaction, but a rigorous bridge is not
established.  Cassini and Juno data are digitized from published figures.
Three configurations from two planets is suggestive but not conclusive
for universality; Uranus and Neptune observations would strengthen the
claim.

\subsection{What is novel}

The individual components---Onsager condensation, Kraichnan cascades,
Thomson stability, 2D Green's functions---are established.  What is new
is the chain connecting them: the closed-form proof that $H''(0) < 0$
for all $N \ge 3$; the sign rule $h' < 0 \Rightarrow H'' < 0$
connecting Green's function monotonicity to polygon selection; the
argument that two-dimensionality forces the entire chain; and the
unified Saturn--Jupiter framework showing both arise from the same
mechanism.
