\section*{Prologue: The Polygon Number}

\subsection*{The hexagon that started this}

Saturn has a hexagon at its north pole. Not a rough hexagonal shape, not
a six-sided approximation---a persistent, symmetric, six-sided polygon
of clouds, each side longer than the diameter of Earth. The Voyager
spacecraft photographed it in 1981. The Cassini orbiter confirmed it
was still there thirty years later, rotating in lockstep with the
planet's interior. Jupiter has an octagon at its south pole: eight
cyclones arranged in a regular ring, each the size of the continental
United States, mapped by the Juno mission starting in 2017.

These are not weather in the way we think of weather. A hurricane on
Earth forms, wanders, and dies. These polygons persist for decades,
possibly centuries. They do not drift. They do not deform. They sit at
the poles and spin.

Why polygons? Why \emph{these} polygons---six for Saturn, eight for
Jupiter? I first encountered the Voyager photos years ago and did what
most people do: marveled, read a few explanations, and filed it under
``Nature Gonna Geometry.'' Crystals, snowflakes, beehives, the
nautilus shell---the physical world converges on geometric forms for
all sorts of reasons. I assumed the hexagon was another entry on that
list.

Years later I was watching an astronomy video with my kids and the
polar polygons came up again. My children were astonished. I was
curious. I dove back into the literature, ordered a copy of
\emph{Geophysical Fluid Dynamics} by Joseph Pedlosky, and started
writing code.

What I found was a formula---published in 1931 by a mathematician
named T.~H.~Havelock---that counts how many spinning objects can
sit in a stable ring. The formula is not complicated. It has two
parts: one from geometry, one from the surface the objects sit on.
The geometry part is universal; it does not care whether the objects
are storms on a gas giant, quantum vortices in superfluid helium, or
anything else that spins logarithmically. The surface part changes
from problem to problem.

On a flat surface, the formula says the largest stable ring has seven
members. Seven. Not six, not eight---seven. Saturn's hexagon has six
sides; Jupiter's octagon has eight. Both are one step away from seven,
on opposite sides.

I became determined to pull the connecting string until the knot
unraveled. The string led from fluid dynamics to algebraic number
theory to general relativity to particle physics to cosmology. I
followed it for years. The result is a six-paper technical series.
This companion document is my attempt to explain what that series
says, and why, without requiring any of the mathematics that makes
it work.


\subsection*{The one idea}

Here is the central claim, stated as plainly as I can manage.

When identical spinning objects arrange themselves in a ring on a
surface, there is a number---call it $N$---that counts how many
objects are in the ring. The ring's stability depends on a single
quantity that splits into two parts. The first part comes from the
surface: flat, curved like a sphere, curved like a saddle. The
second part comes from pure geometry: how many objects there are and
how they interact pairwise.

On a flat surface, the two parts balance exactly at $N = 7$. A ring
of seven is marginally stable---it neither grows nor shrinks when
disturbed. Below seven, the ring snaps back. Above seven, the ring
flies apart.

This number seven is not adjustable. It is not a parameter I chose or
a threshold I fit to data. It falls out of the formula the way four
falls out of the corners of a square. It is a property of the
two-dimensional logarithmic interaction---the mathematical law that
governs how point-like spinning objects push each other around on a
surface.

The technical papers argue that this same threshold determines far
more than atmospheric polygons. The number seven, and the algebraic
structure surrounding it, fixes the spin of the graviton (the
hypothetical particle that carries gravity); forces the gauge group
of particle physics to be $\mathrm{SU}(3) \times \mathrm{SU}(2)
\times \mathrm{U}(1)$ (the symmetry behind the strong, weak, and
electromagnetic forces); and constrains the cosmological constant
(the energy density of empty space). These are not separate
coincidences bolted together. They are consequences of one formula
applied to one threshold.

Whether the argument is correct is for the scientific community to
assess. My goal here is to make it accessible enough that you can
follow the logic, identify where the assumptions are, and form your
own judgment.

\subsection*{The honest disclaimer}

I am not a physicist by training. My background is philosophy with a
mathematical bent; professionally, I am a software developer working
in healthcare. A hobbyist's obsession with number theory is an apt
description of my post-educational years. I am unaffiliated with any
university or research institution.

This matters because the claims in the technical series are
extraordinary, and extraordinary claims face a higher bar. I want to
be transparent about both the strengths and the limitations.

The results fall into three tiers of confidence:

\textbf{Proved.} A small number of results are mathematical theorems.
If you accept the starting identification---that a point vortex on a
two-dimensional surface and a gravitational source share the same
Green's function---then these results follow by logical necessity. The
gauge group of particle physics being
$\mathrm{SU}(3) \times \mathrm{SU}(2) \times \mathrm{U}(1)$ is in
this category. The proof uses finite group theory (specifically, the
symmetry group PSL(2,7) acting on the Klein quartic surface), and the
steps are checkable by anyone with an undergraduate algebra textbook.

\textbf{Derived.} Most results are in this tier. They follow from a
chain of identifications---each step uses standard physics or
standard mathematics---with controlled approximations whose errors
are estimated. The Weinberg angle, the Hubble constant, the quark
mixing parameters, and the Higgs mass are all derived. These results
could be wrong if one of the identifications in the chain fails, but
the chain is explicit: every link is written down, and each can be
tested independently.

\textbf{Conjectured.} A handful of results are numerically verified
but rely on at least one step that is physically motivated rather than
mathematically proved. The dark matter fraction is in this category.
The number matches, but the derivation has a gap that I have flagged
honestly.

Institutional research correctly forces disciplinary depth; the cost
is that cross-domain connections can go unnoticed. I am unaffiliated,
which meant I could follow the mathematics wherever it led, without
the professional incentive to stay within a single field. Whether that
freedom produced something worth the lack of expert supervision is for
the reader to assess.


\subsection*{The map}

The technical series has six papers. Here is what each one does.

\textbf{Paper I: The Mathematics.} This paper establishes the
foundation. The logarithmic interaction---the same law that governs
how spinning fluid parcels push each other---is the unique
two-dimensional interaction for which polygon stability is
scale-independent. The Havelock formula (1931) gives the stability
eigenvalues. On a flat surface the critical threshold is $N = 7$.
On curved surfaces the threshold shifts, and the shift values live
in algebraic number fields: the threshold for the transition from
seven to eight stable vortices is $8 - 3\sqrt{7}$, a unit in the
ring of integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{7})$. This is where the
algebraic number theory enters, and it is not decoration---these
algebraic structures determine the physical predictions downstream.

\textbf{Paper II: The Physics.} This paper connects the mathematics
to actual fluid dynamics. Point vortices have no inertia; they are
pushed sideways by their neighbors. A central vortex can stabilize
rings that would otherwise be unstable. The paper derives the
critical central-vortex strength for each $N$, resolves the $N = 7$
marginal case with a quartic (fourth-order) calculation, and shows
that the stability results survive two realistic corrections: the
finite size of real vortices (the ``blob'' correction, which is
independent of the vortex profile shape) and the Rossby-wave
stationarity constraint that pins Saturn's hexagon at $N = 6$. The
final sections identify a structural isomorphism between point
vortices and gravitational sources: both produce conical
singularities with the same Green's function.

\textbf{Paper III: Gravity.} The marginal mode at $N = 7$ carries
spin 2---the same spin as the graviton, the hypothetical quantum of
gravity. This paper derives Einstein's equation (the field equation
of general relativity) from the statistical mechanics of vortex
configurations, using Jensen's inequality and the Onsager
large-energy limit. It constructs the Wheeler--DeWitt equation (the
quantum gravity wave equation) from the breathing mode of the
polygon, with the central charge $c = 12\,b(N)$ determined by the
Fisher--Rao metric on the space of vortex configurations. Black hole
thermodynamics, holography, and the emergence of quantum mechanics
from statistical mechanics are all addressed.

\textbf{Paper IV: Particle Physics.} The polygon hierarchy forces
the internal geometry to be an eleven-dimensional manifold (four
spacetime dimensions plus a seven-dimensional compact space). The
compact space is rigid: Thurston's geometrization theorem eliminates
all possibilities except one. From that geometry, the Standard Model
gauge group $\mathrm{SU}(3) \times \mathrm{SU}(2) \times
\mathrm{U}(1)$ is derived, not assumed. The Weinberg angle comes
out as $\sin^2\theta_W = 3/11$, a ratio of Casimir invariants. The
CKM phase, quark mass ratios, the Higgs mass, the strong coupling
constant, and the string tension of quark confinement are all
computed from the same central charge.

\textbf{Paper V: Cosmology.} The cosmological constant---the energy
density of empty space---is determined by the instanton action of the
$N = 11$ Born--Oppenheimer tunneling amplitude. The hierarchy between
the Planck scale and the electroweak scale (a factor of $10^{16}$)
is not put in by hand; it emerges from the tunneling exponent. The
cosmic energy budget (dark energy, dark matter, ordinary matter)
decomposes into tree-level, one-loop, and tunneling contributions.
The Hubble constant, the neutrino mass hierarchy, and the initial
conditions of inflation are derived.

\textbf{Paper VI: The Scorecard.} This paper collects every
prediction, classifies each by confidence tier, quantifies the
cumulative uncertainty, and lists six binary pass/fail experimental
tests---results from JUNO, LEGEND, Hyper-Kamiokande, LISA, and
DESI---that can confirm or falsify specific claims. It also
catalogues what remains open: the dark matter production mechanism,
the baryon asymmetry factor, and the light quark masses.

\section{Why logarithms are special}
\noindent\textit{[Technical reference: Paper~I, \S1--2]}

\medskip

\noindent\textit{Bottom line: The logarithmic interaction is the only
law in two dimensions for which polygon stability does not depend on
size.}

\medskip

In three dimensions, gravity falls off as $1/r^2$. Drop to two
dimensions and the natural law becomes the logarithm: the force
between spinning objects falls off as $1/r$.

This is not a modeling choice. It is forced by the mathematics of
the two-dimensional Laplacian---the same equation that governs heat
diffusion, electrostatics, and fluid flow on a flat surface. The
Green's function (the fundamental solution) of that equation is the
logarithm. Period.

What makes the logarithm special among all possible force laws is a
specific kind of rigidity. If you take a polygon governed by
logarithmic forces and zoom in or zoom out---rescale the entire
configuration by a factor $b$---the stability does not change. The
energy shifts by a constant that depends on $b$ but not on the
positions. The equations of motion are identical before and after
rescaling.

Try any other force law. The power law $1/r^2$ (gravity in 3D,
projected onto a plane): zooming out makes the polygon \emph{less}
stable. The interaction weakens faster than the geometry spreads, and
eventually the ring flies apart. A steeper law like $1/r^3$: same
problem, worse. A shallower law like $1/\sqrt{r}$: zooming out makes
the polygon \emph{more} stable, but zooming in destabilizes it. The
logarithm sits exactly at the boundary. It is the unique fixed point.

The technical language for this classification borrows vocabulary from
the renormalization group---a tool from statistical mechanics that
sorts interactions by their behavior under rescaling. An interaction
steeper than the logarithm is ``relevant'': it grows under
coarse-graining and destabilizes at large scales. An interaction
shallower than the logarithm is ``irrelevant'': it shrinks under
coarse-graining and destabilizes at small scales. The logarithm itself
is ``marginal''---scale-invariant. Paper~I proves that this
classification is exhaustive: every two-dimensional pairwise
interaction falls into one of these three categories, and the
logarithm is the unique boundary between them.

One practical consequence: real vortices are not mathematical points.
They have finite cores---a smeared-out region of rotation rather than
an infinitely concentrated singularity. The interaction between two
finite-core vortices at large separation looks like
$-\kappa^2 \ln d + O(\varepsilon^2/d^2)$, where $d$ is the distance,
$\kappa$ is the circulation, and $\varepsilon$ is the core size. The
correction term $O(\varepsilon^2/d^2)$ falls in the ``irrelevant''
category ($1/r^2$, shrinking under rescaling). So at large
separations, any compact vortex distribution flows toward the
logarithmic interaction. The finite-core details wash out.

This is why the stability threshold $N = 7$ (which I have not derived
yet---that comes in Section~4) is not an artifact of an idealized
model. It is the fixed point that real vortex systems approach as
they separate.

\textbf{Status: (Proved)}---this is a mathematical theorem about the
two-dimensional Laplacian and the Cauchy functional equation. The
proof is in Paper~I, \S2.

\section{The sign rule: bowls, not hilltops}
\noindent\textit{[Technical reference: Paper~I, \S3]}

\medskip

\noindent\textit{Bottom line: The regular polygon sits at the bottom
of an energy valley, not balanced on a hilltop.}

\medskip

Before this series, the standard treatment of polygon stability
described the regular $N$-gon as an energy saddle---stable in some
directions of perturbation, unstable in others. Think of sitting on a
horse saddle: rock forward and you slide down; rock sideways and
you're pushed back. A saddle is stable along one axis and unstable
along the other. Most of the point-vortex literature characterized the
polygon this way.

The sign rule (Theorem~3 in Paper~I) corrects this picture. The
regular polygon is a \emph{constrained energy minimum}: a bowl, not a
hilltop, not a saddle.

The analogy: a marble in a bowl. Displace the marble; it rolls back
to the center. A marble on a dome: displace it; it rolls away. The
polygon is the bowl.

There is a subtlety, and I want to be honest about it. The polygon is
a saddle of the \emph{unconstrained} energy---the energy considered as
a function of all possible configurations. If you could grab the
vortices and rearrange them arbitrarily, you would find directions
where the energy decreases. But vortices cannot be rearranged
arbitrarily. They are constrained by a conserved quantity called the
angular impulse (a measure of how much total spin the system carries).
The constraint removes the unstable directions. On the
angular-impulse surface---the set of all configurations with the same
total spin---the polygon sits at a minimum.

The sign rule itself is more general than the logarithm. It applies to
any pairwise interaction $h(r)$ satisfying two conditions: the force
is attractive ($h'(r) < 0$, meaning the interaction pulls objects
toward each other) and the quantity $(r \cdot h'(r))'$ is
non-positive (a concavity condition on the force law). Any interaction
satisfying both conditions produces a polygon that is a constrained
minimum.

The logarithmic interaction $h(r) = -\ln r$ is the borderline case.
It satisfies $h' < 0$ (attractive: $h' = -1/r$) and
$(rh')' = 0$ (the concavity condition is satisfied with
equality, not strict inequality). In the bowl analogy: for most
interactions satisfying the sign rule, the bowl has a standard curved
bottom---displace the marble and it accelerates back. For the
logarithm, the bowl is flat at the very bottom. The restoring force is
not quadratic (proportional to displacement) but quartic
(proportional to displacement cubed). The marble still returns to
center; it just takes longer. It sits in a flat-bottomed bowl.

This flatness at $N = 7$ is not a defect. It is the mathematical
signature of the critical threshold. The flat bottom of the bowl is
precisely where the quartic analysis of Section~4 becomes necessary.

Nature finds energy minima. That is the second law of thermodynamics
in one sentence. The sign rule tells us that the regular polygon
\emph{is} the energy minimum for the constrained system. This is why
polygons form, why they persist, and why they are regular rather than
irregular.

\textbf{Status: (Proved)}---the sign rule is Theorem~3 of Paper~I.
The proof is self-contained and uses only calculus and the properties
of trigonometric sums.

\section{The Havelock formula: one equation, two parts}
\noindent\textit{[Technical reference: Paper~I, \S4]}

\medskip

\noindent\textit{Bottom line: The stability of any polygon on any
surface splits into one number from the surface and one number from
pure counting.}

\medskip

This is the only equation in this companion that I will ask you to
look at:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:havelock-companion}
  \lambda_m \;=\; C_1 \;-\; \frac{m(N-m)}{2}\,.
\end{equation}
Every symbol earns its keep, so let me translate each one.

\begin{itemize}
\item $\lambda_m$ (``lambda-sub-$m$''): the stability of the $m$-th
  wobble mode. If $\lambda_m$ is positive, that mode is stable---the
  polygon resists that particular distortion. If negative, the polygon
  is unstable against it. If zero, the polygon is on the edge: the
  linear restoring force has vanished, and you need to look at
  higher-order terms.

\item $m$: which wobble pattern. $m = 1$ is the gentlest perturbation,
  where the whole ring shifts slightly off-center. $m = 2$ is an
  elliptical distortion. $m = 3$ is a triangular distortion. The index
  $m$ runs from $1$ to $N - 1$, covering all independent ways the
  polygon can deform.

\item $N$: how many objects are in the ring.

\item $C_1$: one single number that encodes everything about the
  surface---flat plane, sphere, hyperbolic plane, whatever. On a flat
  surface, $C_1 = N - 1$. On a sphere, $C_1$ depends on the latitude.
  On a hyperbolic surface, $C_1$ depends on the curvature radius. This
  is the only part of the formula that changes from surface to surface.

\item $m(N-m)/2$: a counting pattern that depends only on $N$ and $m$.
  It is the same on every surface. It comes from the representation
  theory of the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_N$---a precise way of saying
  ``the symmetry of the ring.'' If you place $N$ identical objects
  equally spaced around a circle, the symmetry group of the
  configuration is $\mathbb{Z}_N$ (the group of rotations by multiples
  of $360^\circ/N$). The number $m(N-m)/2$ is the Casimir invariant of the
  $m$-th representation of that group. The name does not matter. What
  matters is that this term is universal.
\end{itemize}

In words: the stability of any polygon on any surface splits cleanly
into a piece from the surface ($C_1$) and a piece from pure counting
($m(N-m)/2$). The counting piece is the same whether the polygon sits
on a flat table, a sphere, or a saddle.

T.~H.~Havelock published this formula in 1931, for the specific case
of a flat surface. What is new in this series is the proof that the
counting part $m(N-m)/2$ is \emph{metric-independent}---it does not
change when you curve the surface. The proof (Paper~I, \S4) proceeds
by showing that the tangential Hessian of the energy is a circulant
matrix with a $\csc^2$ kernel, and the Fourier eigenvalue of that
kernel is exactly $m(N-m)/2$ by an identity in the representation
theory of $\mathbb{Z}_N$. The surface enters only through the radial
part of the Hessian, which collapses into the single number $C_1$.

The separation is clean because the Laplacian on any
constant-curvature surface gives a Green's function that depends only
on geodesic distance. The off-diagonal structure (how vortices
interact) is determined by $\mathbb{Z}_N$; the diagonal structure (how
each vortex sits in its potential well) is determined by the surface.
They do not mix.

This is the architectural fact on which everything else rests. If the
formula did not split, each surface would require a separate stability
analysis. Because it does split, every stability question on every
constant-curvature surface reduces to computing one number: $C_1$.

\textbf{Status: (Proved)}---Theorem~4 in Paper~I. The proof
uses $\mathbb{Z}_N$ Fourier analysis and the Laplacian Green's function.

\section{Why seven is the magic number}
\noindent\textit{[Technical reference: Paper~I, \S4--5]}

\medskip

\noindent\textit{Bottom line: On a flat surface, a ring of seven is
the largest stable polygon. This is a theorem, not a guess.}

\medskip

On a flat surface, $C_1 = N - 1$. Substitute that into the Havelock
formula and the stability question becomes: is
$(N - 1) - m(N - m)/2 \ge 0$ for every mode $m$?

The most dangerous mode---the one closest to going negative---is the
one that maximizes $m(N - m)/2$. That maximum occurs at
$m = \lfloor N/2 \rfloor$: the halfway mode, the perturbation that
splits the polygon into two interleaved groups as far apart as
possible.

Work through the arithmetic for specific values of $N$.

At $N = 6$ (hexagon), the most dangerous mode is $m = 3$:
$\lambda_3 = 5 - 3 \times 3/2 = 5 - 4.5 = 0.5$. Positive. Stable.

At $N = 7$ (heptagon), the most dangerous mode is $m = 3$:
$\lambda_3 = 6 - 3 \times 4/2 = 6 - 6 = 0$. Exactly zero. On the
edge.

At $N = 8$ (octagon), the most dangerous mode is $m = 4$:
$\lambda_4 = 7 - 4 \times 4/2 = 7 - 8 = -1$. Negative. Unstable.

The transition is sharp. There is no $N = 7.5$. Seven is the last
integer where the formula stays non-negative.

Why does $N = 7$ produce zero? Paper~I, Theorem~10 gives the algebraic
answer: setting $\lambda_m = 0$ with $C_1 = N - 1$ and solving gives
$(N - 1)(N - 7) = 0$. The roots are $N = 1$ (below the polygon range)
and $N = 7$. The number seven is not chosen; it is the unique
nontrivial root of a quadratic that arises from the intersection of
the linear function $C_1 = N - 1$ (from dilation invariance) and the
quadratic Casimir $m(N-m)/2$ (from $\mathbb{Z}_N$ representation
theory). The coefficient $8$ in $N^2 - 8N + 7 = 0$ comes from two
factors of the representation theory: the $1/2$ in the Havelock
normalization and the critical-mode evaluation at $m = (N-1)/2$.

There is a loose end at $N = 7$. The eigenvalue $\lambda_3 = 0$ means
the linear restoring force vanishes. The marble in the bowl sits on a
flat bottom. Does it stay put, or does it eventually slide away? To
answer this, you need to check the next term in the expansion: the
quartic (fourth-order) term.

Paper~II computes this quartic coefficient:
$\alpha_0 = 45/14 \approx 3.21 > 0$. Positive. The flat-bottomed
bowl curves back up at fourth order. The marble returns to center; it
just accelerates more slowly than a standard quadratic bowl (quartic
restoring force $\propto x^3$ rather than linear $\propto x$).

The seven-sided polygon is stable, but marginally so. It is the last
polygon that holds together on a flat surface, and it holds together
by the quartic correction rather than the standard linear restoring
force.

\textbf{Status: (Proved)}---$N_{\mathrm{crit}} = 7$ is Theorem~10 of
Paper~I. The quartic resolution $\alpha_0 = 45/14 > 0$ is proved in
Paper~II.

\section{Curved surfaces and algebraic number theory}
\noindent\textit{[Technical reference: Paper~I, \S5--6]}

\medskip

\noindent\textit{Bottom line: On curved surfaces, the stability
threshold shifts---and the shift values live in algebraic number
fields tied to $\sqrt{7}$.}

\medskip

On a flat surface, $C_1 = N - 1$ and the threshold is $N = 7$. On a
curved surface, $C_1$ changes and the threshold moves.

The sphere and the hyperbolic plane push in opposite directions.

\subsection*{The sphere: curvature destabilizes}

On a sphere of radius $a$, with the polygon sitting at colatitude
$\varphi_0$ (the angle from the north pole), the curvature coefficient
is $C_1 = (N-1)\cos\varphi_0$. At the pole ($\varphi_0 = 0$),
$\cos\varphi_0 = 1$ and you recover the flat result $C_1 = N - 1$.
As the polygon moves toward the equator, $\cos\varphi_0$ decreases,
$C_1$ drops, and the polygon destabilizes at smaller $N$.

The thresholds are all rational numbers. For a triangle ($N = 3$):
the critical colatitude is $60^\circ$, corresponding to
$\xi_{\mathrm{crit}} = 1/3$. For a square ($N = 4$): $48.2^\circ$,
$\xi_{\mathrm{crit}} = 1/5$. For a pentagon ($N = 5$): $41.4^\circ$,
$\xi_{\mathrm{crit}} = 1/7$. For a hexagon ($N = 6$): $25.8^\circ$,
$\xi_{\mathrm{crit}} = 1/19$. For a heptagon ($N = 7$): $0^\circ$---the
exact pole, and only marginally stable there. For $N \ge 8$: no
stable latitude exists on the sphere, at all.

The rationality of these thresholds is not obvious. The Green's
function on the sphere involves transcendental functions (logarithms
of sines). But $C_1$ on the sphere depends linearly on
$\cos\varphi_0$, and the marginal condition $C_1 = m(N-m)/2$ produces
a rational equation. Transcendental interaction, rational threshold.

\subsection*{The hyperbolic plane: curvature stabilizes}

On the hyperbolic plane (constant negative curvature, like a saddle
that extends in all directions), the opposite happens. The curvature
coefficient is
$C_1 = (N-1)(1 + \xi^2)/(1-\xi)^2$,
where $\xi = r_E^2/a^2$ measures how far the polygon sits from the
center of the Poincar\'e disk (the standard model of the hyperbolic
plane). Since $(1 + \xi^2)/(1-\xi)^2 > 1$ for any $\xi > 0$, we have
$C_1 > N - 1$: negative curvature \emph{boosts} $C_1$ above its flat
value. Polygons that would fly apart on a flat surface can be held
together by sufficient negative curvature.

The threshold for the $7 \to 8$ transition---the curvature at which an
octagon first becomes stable---is
$\xi^* = 8 - 3\sqrt{7} \approx 0.063$.
This number comes from solving the equation
$\xi^2 - 16\xi + 1 = 0$.

I want to draw attention to this equation. It reads the same forwards
and backwards: the coefficient of $\xi^2$ (which is $1$) equals the
constant term (also $1$). This palindromic structure forces the two
roots to be reciprocals of each other: if $\xi^*$ is a root, then
$1/\xi^*$ is the other root. The smaller root is the stability
threshold; the larger root is its conformal inverse.

The verification: $(8 - 3\sqrt{7})(8 + 3\sqrt{7}) = 64 - 63 = 1$.
The product of the two roots is exactly $1$, confirming the
palindromic structure.

The number $8 - 3\sqrt{7}$ is the inverse of the fundamental unit
of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{7}]$---the ring of integers in the number field
$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{7})$. The fundamental unit is
$\varepsilon = 8 + 3\sqrt{7}$; its inverse is
$\varepsilon^{-1} = 8 - 3\sqrt{7}$. These are standard objects in
algebraic number theory: every quadratic number field has a
fundamental unit, and the Pell equation $x^2 - 7y^2 = 1$ (solved by
$x = 8$, $y = 3$) is how you find it.

The palindromic structure is not a coincidence. It encodes the
conformal inversion symmetry $\xi \leftrightarrow 1/\xi$ of the
Poincar\'e disk model---the same M\"obius covariance that makes the
logarithmic energy special (Section~1). The stability threshold is an
algebraic unit \emph{because} the hyperbolic energy is conformally
covariant.

\subsection*{The field extension pattern}

The pattern generalizes. For each $N \ge 8$, there is a threshold
$\xi^*(N)$ at which the $N$-gon first becomes stable on the hyperbolic
plane. Each threshold lives in a quadratic number field determined by
a simple rule:
\begin{itemize}
\item For even $N$: the field is
  $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\mathrm{squarefree}(N-1)})$.
\item For odd $N$: the field is
  $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\mathrm{squarefree}(N-3)})$.
\end{itemize}
Here ``squarefree'' means: divide out all perfect-square factors. So
$\mathrm{squarefree}(9) = 1$ (since $9 = 3^2$), and
$\mathrm{squarefree}(12) = 3$ (since $12 = 4 \times 3$).

The special case $N = 10$: $\mathrm{squarefree}(9) = 1$, so the
threshold lives in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{1}) = \mathbb{Q}$. It is a plain
rational number: $\xi^*(10) = 1/7$. The number~$7$ appearing here is
the flat-plane threshold making a cameo in the hyperbolic setting.

\subsection*{The five algebraic integers}

Among all the thresholds, exactly five are algebraic integers---numbers
that satisfy a monic polynomial equation with integer coefficients:
$N = 8$, $9$, $11$, $15$, and $23$. The sequence of gaps between them
is $1$, $2$, $4$, $8$---powers of two. This is not numerology; it
follows from the divisibility condition $(N - 7) \mid 16$, which comes
directly from the structure of the palindromic equation.

The last algebraic-integer threshold is at $N = 23$:
$\xi^*(23) = \varphi^{-2} = (3 - \sqrt{5})/2 \approx 0.382$,
where $\varphi = (1 + \sqrt{5})/2$ is the golden ratio. Its
palindromic equation is $\xi^2 - 3\xi + 1 = 0$, with trace $c = 3$.
The trace $c = 2$ would give a degenerate equation ($(\xi - 1)^2 = 0$),
so $c = 3$ is the smallest trace compatible with two distinct positive
roots. The golden ratio is the algebraically simplest non-trivial
palindromic unit. The sequence terminates here; no further
algebraic-integer thresholds exist beyond $N = 23$.

\textbf{Status: (Proved)}---the field extension pattern, the
algebraic-integer classification, and the golden ratio terminal case
are all proved in Paper~I, \S6.

\section{The stability phase diagram}
\noindent\textit{[Technical reference: Paper~I, \S7]}

\medskip

\noindent\textit{Bottom line: The stability boundaries form a
topological phase diagram with sharp, undeformable transitions.}

\medskip

Combine everything above. Each surface has its own $C_1$. Each $N$
has its own critical mode. Together they carve the $(N, C_1)$ plane
into stability zones. Inside a zone, the polygon is stable. Cross a
boundary, it is not.

These boundaries are not fuzzy. They are not approximate or subject
to perturbative corrections. They are sharp---a mathematical fact. The
Morse index (the count of unstable modes) is an integer, and it jumps
by exactly one at each boundary. At $N = 7$ on a flat surface, the
Morse index is zero (no unstable modes). At $N = 8$, it jumps to
three: three independent wobble patterns ($m = 3$, $4$, and $5$) are
simultaneously unstable. For general $N \ge 8$, the Morse index is
$N - 5$.

As the curvature parameter $\xi$ increases from $0$ (flat) toward $1$
(infinite negative curvature), each unstable mode crosses zero
\emph{exactly once}---it goes from negative (unstable) to positive
(stable). The crossings happen at the palindromic thresholds:
$\xi^*(8) = 8 - 3\sqrt{7}$, then $\xi^*(9) = 5 - 2\sqrt{6}$, then
$\xi^*(10) = 1/7$, and so on, each value sitting in its own algebraic
number field. The total number of crossings (the spectral flow) equals
the flat-plane Morse index $N - 5$.

Paper~I, Appendix~A shows that these stability phases have a
topological character. The technical tool is $K$-theory---a branch of
algebra that classifies objects by properties that cannot be smoothly
deformed away. In the same way that a sphere and a torus are
topologically distinct (you cannot deform one into the other without
tearing), the stability zones on either side of a palindromic
threshold are $K$-theoretically distinct. The Morse index
$[\mu_N(\xi)]$ defines a class in $K_0(C^*(\mathbb{Z}_N))$, the
$K$-theory group of the symmetry algebra. That class is locally
constant: it does not change under small perturbations. It changes
only when an eigenvalue crosses zero---at a palindromic threshold.

The practical consequence: the boundaries cannot be smoothed away. If
you perturb the system---change the interaction slightly, add a small
asymmetry, introduce a bounded coupling between Fourier modes---the
boundaries shift, but they do not disappear. The Morse index is
topologically protected. Standard Fredholm theory guarantees that any
bounded perturbation with $V(0) = 0$ preserves the spectral flow.

On quotient surfaces (the hyperbolic plane modded out by a discrete
group, producing a compact surface with higher genus), the thresholds
acquire a correction from the surface topology. The correction is
computed by the Selberg trace formula---the same tool that counts
prime geodesics. Each non-trivial geodesic on the surface contributes
a term to $C_1$ whose strength is proportional to
$\operatorname{csch}^2(\ell/2)$, where $\ell$ is the geodesic
length. Short geodesics contribute more; long geodesics are
exponentially suppressed.

On the Bolza surface---a particular compact hyperbolic surface of
genus $2$ with the largest automorphism group ($48$ elements) of any
genus-$2$ surface---the correction is small: $|\delta C_1| < 0.011$.
The fractional Morse index at the palindromic threshold is
$\tau(P_-) = 0.644$, meaning roughly $64\%$ of the stability weight
is in the unstable direction. This number is computed from the
Fuchsian group of the surface, bounded rigorously by the Selberg
trace formula, and verified numerically.

The stability phase diagram, taken as a whole, is a map of the
$(N, \xi)$ plane divided into regions by algebraic curves. The curves
are palindromic polynomials. The regions are labeled by Morse indices.
The labels are topological invariants. And the algebra connecting
surface to surface---the field extensions, the Pell units, the
geodesic correspondence---is not decorative. It is the scaffolding
that holds the later papers together.

\textbf{Status: (Proved)}---the spectral flow, the $K_0$ index
pairing, and the Bolza bound are proved in Paper~I, Appendix~A.
The higher-genus palindromic hierarchy (Conjecture~1 in Paper~I)
remains a conjecture.

\section{How vortices actually move}
\noindent\textit{[Technical reference: Paper~II, \S1--3]}

\medskip

\noindent\textit{Bottom line: Point vortices have no inertia---they go
wherever the other vortices push them.}

\medskip

A vortex is not a particle. It is a localized patch of spinning fluid:
a whirlpool, a cyclone, a tornado. It has no mass. It does not
accelerate. Drop a ball and gravity pulls it; the ball gains speed,
picks up momentum, resists being stopped. A vortex does none of this.
It moves at whatever velocity the surrounding flow dictates, instantly,
with no lag.

This is the single most important fact about vortex dynamics, and it is
the fact that makes vortex systems fundamentally different from the
particle systems physicists usually study. Newton's second law says
$F = ma$: force equals mass times acceleration. For a vortex, $m = 0$.
There is no second derivative. The equation of motion is first-order:
velocity, not acceleration. The vortex goes where the flow tells it to
go, right now, with no memory of where it was heading a moment ago.

The equations governing $N$ point vortices on a surface are Hamiltonian
---they conserve energy---but the Hamiltonian plays a dual role. In
ordinary mechanics, the Hamiltonian generates the time evolution of
both positions \emph{and} momenta. For vortices, there are no momenta.
The positions are the entire state. The Hamiltonian (the energy) is a
function of the positions alone, and it directly prescribes the
velocities.

What is that energy? The interaction between two point vortices on a
flat surface is the logarithm of their separation:
$H = -\sum_{j<k}\ln|z_j - z_k|$. Each pair contributes. If you have
six vortices, there are fifteen pairs; each pair pulls logarithmically.
The total energy is the sum. On a curved surface---a sphere, a
hyperbolic plane---the logarithm is replaced by the Green's function
of the Laplacian on that surface, but the structure is the same: a sum
of pairwise interactions, each determined by the surface geometry.

Three quantities are conserved. The energy $H$ itself. The angular
impulse $L = \sum|z_k|^2$, which measures the total spin-weighted
spread of the vortices around the origin---think of it as a rotational
moment of inertia. And the linear impulse $\mathbf{P} = \sum z_k$, the
center of vorticity. These conservation laws constrain the motion.
The vortices cannot wander freely through configuration space; they are
confined to the surface defined by $\{L = \text{constant},\;
\mathbf{P} = 0\}$. That surface has $2N - 3$ dimensions (start with
$2N$ coordinates, subtract three constraints).

The regular $N$-gon---$N$ identical vortices equally spaced around a
circle---is a critical point of the energy on this constraint surface.
Paper~I proved it is a \emph{minimum}, not a maximum or saddle. Paper~II
makes this into physics: the polygon is the equilibrium configuration
that a vortex system naturally settles into. It is the bottom of the
bowl.

There is a specific deformation path through configuration space that
Paper~II analyzes in detail: the centered logarithmic spiral. Start
with the regular $N$-gon. Deform it so the vortices spiral inward,
each one shifted slightly toward or away from the center in a pattern
that respects the $\mathbb{Z}_N$ symmetry. This spiral is the unique
$\mathbb{Z}_N$-equivariant perturbation tangent to a M\"obius orbit
---a mouthful, but the idea is straightforward: it is the most
symmetric way to deform the polygon while keeping the overall rotational
pattern intact.

Along this spiral, the energy curvature $H''(0)$ is strictly negative.
The polygon sits at a maximum of the unconstrained energy along the
spiral direction. But the spiral does not stay on the constraint
surface---it violates the angular-impulse conservation at second order.
Once you project back onto the constraint surface (the physically
accessible configurations), the maximum becomes a minimum. The
Lagrange multiplier from the angular-impulse constraint flips the sign.
This is the core of the constrained-minimum result: the polygon is
simultaneously an unconstrained saddle and a constrained minimum. Both
statements are true; they describe different slices through the energy
landscape.

The Onsager statistical-mechanics picture operates on the unconstrained
landscape, where negative temperatures drive vortices to cluster into
the highest-energy configurations accessible at given conserved
quantities. The constrained-minimum picture operates on the physical
surface. The two viewpoints are complementary: Onsager explains
\emph{why} vortices end up at the polygon (it is the energetically
favored state at negative temperature); the constrained Hessian
explains \emph{why} the polygon is stable once they get there.

\textbf{Status: (Proved)}---the constrained-minimum result is
Theorem~1 of Paper~II, using the Havelock eigenvalue identity from
Paper~I.

\section{Stabilizing the unstable: central vortices and the
\texorpdfstring{$N=7$}{N=7} bifurcation}
\noindent\textit{[Technical reference: Paper~II, \S4--5]}

\medskip

\noindent\textit{Bottom line: For eight or more vortices, a strong
central vortex can rescue an otherwise unstable polygon.}

\medskip

At $N = 8$, the ring polygon on a flat surface is unstable. The most
dangerous wobble mode ($m = 4$, the one that splits the octagon into
two interleaved squares) has eigenvalue $\lambda_4 = 7 - 8 = -1$.
Negative. The polygon flies apart.

But suppose there is a central vortex---a strong spinning core sitting
at the exact center of the ring. Every planet with a polar polygon has
one: Jupiter has a massive central cyclone at each pole, surrounded by
the ring of smaller cyclones. The central vortex adds a positive shift
to every eigenvalue. If it is strong enough, it pushes every eigenvalue
back above zero.

Two effects contribute to this shift. First, the Lagrange multiplier
changes: the central vortex alters the constraint surface, and the
angular-impulse condition shifts in a way that adds $+\kappa_0$ to each
eigenvalue, where $\kappa_0$ is the circulation of the central vortex.
Second, the central vortex itself exerts a radial force on each ring
vortex; the Hessian of this interaction projected onto the critical
eigenvector adds another $+\kappa_0$. Total shift: $+2\kappa_0$.

Setting the shifted eigenvalue to zero and solving gives the critical
central-vortex strength. The formulas are clean:
\begin{itemize}
\item For even $N$: $\kappa_{\mathrm{crit}} = (N^2 - 8N + 8)/16$.
\item For odd $N$: $\kappa_{\mathrm{crit}} = (N-1)(N-7)/16$.
\end{itemize}
The first few values: for $N \le 7$, the critical strength is zero---no
central vortex needed. For $N = 8$: $\kappa_{\mathrm{crit}} = 1/2$.
For $N = 9$: $\kappa_{\mathrm{crit}} = 1$. For $N = 10$: $7/4$.
Each step demands a stronger central vortex.

Paper~II also proves that multi-ring configurations---two or more
concentric polygons---cannot achieve what a single central vortex can.
If the outer ring is stable ($N \le 7$), adding an inner ring of
vortices \emph{destabilizes} it. If the outer ring is already unstable
($N \ge 8$), an inner ring reduces but never eliminates the
instability. Only a single vortex at the $\mathbb{Z}_N$ fixed point
(the dead center) provides the uniform eigenvalue shift needed for full
stabilization. This is the center-ring dichotomy, and it matches
observation: Jupiter's south pole has eight cyclones in a ring with one
central cyclone, not two nested rings.

\subsection*{The BEC prediction}

In a Bose--Einstein condensate---an ultracold quantum gas where atoms
occupy the same quantum state---vortices carry quantized circulation.
The circulation is an integer multiple of $h/m_{\mathrm{atom}}$
(Planck's constant divided by the atomic mass). You cannot have half a
quantum of circulation.

This quantization turns the continuous threshold
$\kappa_{\mathrm{crit}} = 1/2$ into a sharp, integer-valued prediction.
A ring of eight unit-winding vortices in a BEC has
$\kappa_{\mathrm{crit}} = 1/2$. The smallest possible central vortex
has winding number $q_0 = 1$, which exceeds $1/2$. So: a ring of eight
vortices should be unstable alone and stable upon adding a single central
vortex. No free parameters. No fitting. The healing length of the
condensate (the ``blob width'' of the vortex core) is so small compared
to the ring radius that the correction from finite core size is less
than one part in ten thousand.

For $N = 9$: $\kappa_{\mathrm{crit}} = 1$, so a single central vortex
with $q_0 = 1$ is marginally stable; solid stability requires $q_0 \ge 2$.
For $N = 10$: $\kappa_{\mathrm{crit}} = 7/4$, so $q_0 = 1$ fails and
$q_0 \ge 2$ is needed. Each step up in $N$ demands a stronger
central vortex, and the quantization of circulation makes the
predictions binary: stable or not, with no wiggle room.

\subsection*{The $N = 7$ quartic resolution}

The case $N = 7$ sits at the boundary. The minimum eigenvalue is
$\lambda_3 = 0$: the linear restoring force has vanished. To determine
stability, Paper~II computes the next term in the Taylor expansion---the
quartic (fourth-order) coefficient. If this coefficient is positive,
the marble sits in a flat-bottomed bowl that curves back up; if
negative, the flat bottom tilts away and the marble slides off.

The computation is exact. No numerical approximation, no floating-point
rounding---exact rational arithmetic throughout. The positions of the
seven vortices are seventh roots of unity (complex numbers on the unit
circle), the interaction is the logarithm, and every intermediate
quantity is an algebraic number. The Galois group $\mathbb{Z}_7$ acts on
these algebraic numbers and forces the final answer to be rational (a
$\mathbb{Z}_7$-invariant sum of elements in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_7)$
must lie in the fixed field $\mathbb{Q}$).

The unconstrained quartic coefficient is $153/7$. The constraint-surface
correction comes from a log-scaling identity: projecting back onto the
angular-impulse surface produces an additive correction of $-18/7$. The
constrained quartic is therefore $153/7 - 18/7 = 135/7$, and the
normal-form coefficient is $\alpha_0 = (135/7)/6 = 45/14 \approx 3.21$.

Positive. The flat-bottomed bowl curves back up. The heptagon is stable.

The physical consequence: the $N = 7$ polygon has a quartic restoring
force ($\propto x^3$) instead of the standard linear restoring force
($\propto x$). Perturb the hexagon, and it snaps back quickly---linear
spring. Perturb the heptagon, and it drifts back slowly---the
amplitude-dependent oscillation frequency vanishes as the displacement
goes to zero. In a BEC experiment, this would show up as an oscillation
period that grows without bound as the perturbation amplitude shrinks, a
signature distinct from the constant-period oscillation of the hexagon.
The predicted frequency for $^{87}$Rb in a $50\;\mu\text{m}$ trap:
$\omega \approx 24\,\varepsilon^2$ per second, where $\varepsilon$ is
the fractional radial displacement. At $5\%$ displacement, the period
is about $100$ seconds---measurable with current BEC imaging.

\textbf{Status: (Proved)}---the central-vortex threshold is
Proposition~4 of Paper~II. The quartic coefficient
$\alpha_0 = 45/14 > 0$ is proved in exact rational arithmetic.
The BEC predictions are \textbf{(Derived)}---they follow from the
quantization of circulation in a condensate.

\section{From perfect to realistic}
\noindent\textit{[Technical reference: Paper~II, \S6--9]}

\medskip

\noindent\textit{Bottom line: The idealized results survive every
realistic correction---finite cores, planetary rotation, and random
circulation variations.}

\medskip

Everything so far treats vortices as mathematical points: zero size,
infinite vorticity concentration, a singularity at the center. Real
vortices---storms on Jupiter, quantum vortices in a condensate---have
finite cores. Saturn's hexagonal jet is not a ring of point vortices;
it is a continuous undulation of a zonal wind. The circulations of
Jupiter's eight polar cyclones are not identical; Juno imagery shows
peak wind speeds ranging from $60$ to $100\;\text{m/s}$ and diameters
from $4{,}000$ to $7{,}000\;\text{km}$.

Paper~II addresses each of these objections with a separate bridge
between the idealized theory and the physical system.

\subsection*{The blob bridge: finite vortex cores}

Replace each point vortex with a smooth ``blob''---a Gaussian bump, a
Cauchy profile, any smooth distribution of vorticity with a finite core
radius $\varepsilon$. The interaction between two blobs at distance $d$
is not exactly $-\ln d$; it picks up a correction of order
$\varepsilon^2/d^2$. The question is whether this correction changes
the stability.

Paper~II proves that it does not, at leading order. The constrained
eigenvalues shift by $O(\varepsilon^2)$, and the shift has two pieces:
a universal Lagrange-multiplier shift $-(N^2 - 1)/12$ (the same for
every Fourier mode, coming from the fact that the equilibrium itself
shifts when the blobs have finite size) and a mode-dependent Hessian
projection $P_m$ (from the modified pairwise interaction). Both pieces
are exact rational numbers, computed in closed form.

The Cauchy blob is the slowest-converging profile---it has algebraic
tails ($\sim r^{-2}$) and infinite second moment, so its Fourier
transform decays at rate $\varepsilon$ rather than $\varepsilon^2$.
Any profile with a finite second moment (Gaussian, compact support,
anything smoother than Cauchy) converges \emph{faster}. So the Cauchy
blob is the worst case, and the stability conclusions proved for
Cauchy carry over \emph{a fortiori} to all smoother profiles. This
profile independence is not a lucky accident; it follows from the
structure of the Fourier transform at low wavenumber.

Three specific consequences. For $N \le 5$: the eigenvalue correction
is $c_m = -1$ (the Lagrange shift dominates), so the eigenvalues
decrease by $\varepsilon^2$, but since the point-vortex eigenvalue is
at least $1$, stability holds for any $\varepsilon < 1$. For $N = 6$:
$c_m > 0$, so the blob correction actually \emph{enhances} stability---
the hexagon is more stable with finite cores than with point vortices.
For $N = 7$: $c_m = +4$, so the marginal eigenvalue
$\lambda_3 = 0 + 4\varepsilon^2 = 4\varepsilon^2 > 0$ for any nonzero
core size. The marginal stability of the point-vortex heptagon is an
artifact of the zero-size limit; any finite core stabilizes it.

The full infinite-dimensional analysis (Appendix~A of Paper~II) goes
further: it shows that the blob \emph{shape} deformations---internal
wobbles of the vortex core---are unconditionally stable. The self-energy
of a mean-zero shape perturbation scales as $1/\varepsilon^2$ (by a
Poincar\'e inequality), so shape modes are stiff; the coupling between
shape and center-of-mass degrees of freedom is quadrupole-order
($O(\varepsilon^2/d^3)$) and vanishes in the point-vortex limit. No
eigenvalue is lost, no sign is changed, no new instability arises from
the infinite-dimensional shape freedom. The point-vortex stability
eigenvalues control the full problem.

\subsection*{The Rossby bridge: Saturn's hexagon}

Saturn's hexagon is not a ring of discrete cyclones. It is a standing
wave on a zonal jet---a continuous ribbon of wind that undulates into
six lobes. The mechanism connecting this wave to the point-vortex
picture is the Rossby bridge.

On a rotating planet, the vorticity gradient $\beta$ (from the
variation of the Coriolis force with latitude) supports Rossby waves
---large-scale meanders of the jet stream. A stationary Rossby wave
has azimuthal wavenumber
$n^* = R\cos\varphi\,\sqrt{\beta_{\mathrm{eff}}/U_{\max}}$,
where $R\cos\varphi$ is the ring radius at latitude $\varphi$,
$U_{\max}$ is the peak jet speed, and $\beta_{\mathrm{eff}}$ includes
both the planetary vorticity gradient and the jet curvature. For Saturn
at $76^\circ$N, the jet curvature dominates $\beta$ by a factor of
about thirteen. Plugging in Cassini-measured values:
$n^* \approx 5.4$ from the leading-order dispersion relation. The
nearest integer is $6$---the wave must fit an integer number of
wavelengths around the planet. (The discrepancy between $5.4$ and $6$
is within the measurement uncertainty of the jet width; a $15\%$
narrower jet gives $n^* = 6.0$ exactly.)

The connection to the point-vortex energy landscape proceeds through
the critical layer of the saturated wave. At finite amplitude, the
stationary Rossby wave develops a cat's-eye structure: vorticity is
trapped into $N$ lobes, each a spinning patch of fluid near a vertex
of the polygon. As the jet sharpens, these lobes concentrate into $N$
isolated patches. In the Prandtl--Batchelor limit (the physically
relevant model for Saturn), the vorticity is constant inside each lobe
and constant outside; the separatrix between lobes carries zero
circulation. The lobes \emph{are} the vortices, and the blob bridge
proves that their finite size does not change the stability
eigenvalues.

Both planets arrive at the same energy landscape through different
limiting procedures. Jupiter concentrates $N$ discrete cyclones into
point vortices (blob radius $\varepsilon \to 0$). Saturn concentrates a
continuous jet meander into $N$ patches (jet width $\sigma \to 0$).
Both limits converge to the Havelock spectrum.

The dimensionless number $\beta R^3/\kappa$ separates the two regimes.
When $\beta R^3/\kappa \ll 1$, vortex interactions dominate Rossby-wave
propagation: this is Jupiter ($\beta R^3/\kappa \approx 0.6$). When
$\beta R^3/\kappa \gg 1$, the wave mechanism dominates: this is Saturn
($\beta R^3/\kappa \approx 7.6$).

\subsection*{The deformation radius: stratification effects}

On a rotating planet with density stratification, the vortex
interaction is modified by the Rossby deformation radius $L_R$. The
Green's function transitions from $-\ln r$ (at separations much less
than $L_R$) to exponential decay $K_0(r/L_R)$ (at separations much
greater than $L_R$). The exponential tail destroys long-range
correlations between non-adjacent vortices, which are what hold the
polygon together.

The polygon-suppression transition: for $N \le 5$, the polygon is
stable at any ratio $R/L_R$. For $N = 6$, the polygon is stable only
if $R/L_R < 1.29$---the ring radius must be less than about $1.3$
deformation radii. For $N = 7$, any finite $R/L_R$ destabilizes the
marginally stable heptagon. For $N \ge 8$, the polygon is already
unstable in the barotropic limit.

Saturn's hexagon persists because Saturn's barotropic column runs deep.
Cassini Grand Finale gravity measurements constrain the differential
rotation to a depth of $8{,}000$--$9{,}000\;\text{km}$, implying a
coherent column of order $10^4\;\text{km}$---far exceeding the
hexagon's vertical scale. The effective deformation radius is large
enough that $R/L_R$ stays below the critical ratio.

Jupiter's situation is less clean. Juno gravity measurements constrain
the differential rotation to about $3{,}000\;\text{km}$ depth ($4\%$ of
Jupiter's radius), far shallower than Saturn's. Whether Jupiter's polar
cyclones sit on deep barotropic Taylor columns or in a shallow weather
layer is not yet settled. The $N = 8$ prediction requires the deep
limit ($R/L_R \lesssim 0.8$, i.e.\ $L_R > 9{,}700\;\text{km}$). A
direct measurement of the depth of the polar cyclone roots would
resolve this; the computation sets a quantitative target.

\subsection*{Circulation disorder: imperfect vortices}

Jupiter's eight polar cyclones are not identical. Their peak wind speeds
vary by a factor of nearly two; their diameters range by $75\%$. The
coefficient of variation in circulation is roughly $15$--$25\%$.
Saturn's hexagonal jet is more uniform (about $5\%$ variation).

Paper~II models this as random perturbations:
$\kappa_k = \kappa(1 + \eta_k)$, where $\eta_k$ is drawn from a
Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma_\eta$. Monte Carlo
simulations over $1{,}000$ realizations compute the probability that
the minimum eigenvalue drops below zero.

The result: the stability domain is a domain, not a knife-edge.
For $N = 6$ at physically relevant disorder ($\sigma_\eta \le 0.2$),
the probability of instability stays below $10\%$. For $N = 8$ on the
hyperbolic plane (curvature-stabilized), the probability stays below
$15\%$ at Jupiter's estimated disorder level. The $N = 7$ marginal ring
is more sensitive---the marginal mode is the first to be destabilized---
but even there, at Jupiter's estimated $\sigma_\eta \approx 0.2$, a
hypothetical $N = 7$ ring would have essentially zero probability of
instability. The sensitivity appears only at unphysically large
disorder ($\sigma_\eta \ge 0.5$).

Configurations comfortably inside the stable region remain stable under
the circulation fluctuations observed at Jupiter and Saturn. The
$N_{\mathrm{crit}}$ boundary holds up.

\textbf{Status: (Proved)}---the blob bridge and profile independence are
mathematical theorems (Proposition~5 of Paper~II). The Rossby bridge
Saturn prediction is \textbf{(Derived)}---it depends on Saturn's
measured jet parameters. The circulation disorder results are

\section{Vortices meet gravity}
\noindent\textit{[Technical reference: Paper~II, \S10--12]}

\medskip

\noindent\textit{Bottom line: In $2{+}1$ dimensional gravity, a point
mass and a point vortex are the same mathematical object.}

\medskip

This section is the bridge to everything that follows in Papers~III
through VI. The claim is precise, so I want to state it carefully.

In $2+1$ dimensional general relativity---gravity in a universe with
two spatial dimensions plus time---a point mass does something
peculiar. It does not create a gravitational well in the Newtonian
sense; there is no $1/r$ force, no gravitational waves, no propagating
degrees of freedom. What it creates is a \emph{conical deficit angle}.
Take a flat sheet of paper. Cut out a wedge. Tape the edges together.
The result is a cone: flat everywhere except at the tip, where the
angular range is less than $360^\circ$. The missing angle is the
deficit. For a point mass $m$ in $2+1$D gravity, the deficit angle is
$\varepsilon = 8\pi G m$, proportional to the mass.

A point vortex on a two-dimensional surface creates the same kind of
singularity in the stream function. The circulation $\kappa$ plays
the same role as the deficit angle. Both objects satisfy the same
equation: the Poisson equation for the two-dimensional Laplacian,
$\nabla^2\Phi = (\text{source strength})\cdot\delta(z - z_0)$.
Both have the logarithm as their Green's function.

The identification is: deficit angle $= 2\pi\kappa$, or equivalently,
$\kappa = 8\pi Gm/(2\pi) = 4Gm$. Once you make this substitution, the
vortex Hamiltonian $H = -\sum_{j<k}\ln|z_j - z_k|$ and the
gravitational interaction energy
$E_{\mathrm{grav}} = -(8\pi G)^2\sum_{j<k}m^2 G_S(z_j, z_k)$ are
the same function up to a positive multiplicative constant. The
equations of motion are the same differential equations. The Havelock
eigenvalues are the same numbers. The stability thresholds---$N = 7$
on a flat surface, the palindromic hierarchy on curved surfaces---are
the same thresholds.

I want to be precise about what this identification is and is not. It
is not an analogy. It is not a metaphor. It is not a qualitative
resemblance. The two systems share the same Green's function, the same
Poisson equation, and therefore the same energy landscape up to overall
scale. The Hessian matrices are proportional; the eigenvalues are
proportional; the stability boundaries are identical. Anything proved
about the vortex system transfers verbatim to the gravitational system,
and vice versa.

There is a difference in dynamics. Vortex equations of motion are
first-order: the Hamiltonian directly prescribes velocity. Gravitational
equations of motion are second-order: there is kinetic energy, there is
potential energy, and the Hamiltonian generates acceleration. But the
stability criterion---whether the polygon is an energy extremum---depends
only on the \emph{potential energy landscape}, not on the kinetic term.
A positive eigenvalue means stable oscillation in the gravitational case
and stable circulation in the vortex case. A negative eigenvalue means
exponential growth in both. The criterion $N \le N_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is
identical.

Paper~II also addresses the backreaction question. Each point mass
creates a conical deficit; does the accumulation of deficit angles
distort the geometry enough to invalidate the flat-plane calculation?
The answer involves the \emph{developing map}---a conformal isometry
from the cone surface to the Euclidean plane, constructed using a
hypergeometric function. The key result: the developing map preserves
$\mathbb{Z}_N$ symmetry (the image of the $N$-gon is still a regular
$N$-gon), and the two-dimensional Green's function is conformally
invariant ($G(z,w) = -(2\pi)^{-1}\log|z - w|$ in conformal coordinates,
regardless of the cone angles). Consequently, the Havelock eigenvalues
are $(N-1) - m(N-m)/2$ for all deficit angles in the allowed range
$Gm \in [0, 1/(2N))$. The stability threshold $N = 7$ does not shift.
The deficit angles do not backreact on the stability.

At the endpoint $Gm = 1/(2N)$, the total deficit exhausts the curvature
budget and the spatial slice compactifies to a sphere. On the sphere,
$C_1 = (N-1)\cos\varphi < N - 1$, and $N_{\mathrm{crit}}$ drops to $6$
or below. The transition from $N_{\mathrm{crit}} = 7$ (flat) to
$N_{\mathrm{crit}} \le 6$ (sphere) is discontinuous---it happens at the
topology change, not through a gradual weakening. The cosmological
constant $\Lambda$ (positive for the sphere, negative for the hyperbolic
plane) controls the stability threshold through $C_1$; the deficit
angles do not.

The convergence proof in Appendix~A closes the last gap: it shows that
the infinite-dimensional fluid system (continuous vorticity fields)
converges to the finite-dimensional point-vortex system in the
appropriate limit. The Hessian of the full Euler energy, including
blob shape deformations and center-shape coupling, has the same
inertia (same count of positive, zero, and negative eigenvalues) as
the point-vortex Havelock spectrum. No information is lost in the
idealization. The point-vortex model is not a cartoon; it is the
correct leading-order description.

\textbf{Status: (Proved)}---the vortex-gravity identification
(Proposition~8 of Paper~II), conformal invariance of flat-plane
stability (Proposition~9), and the convergence theorem
(Proposition~10, Appendix~A) are all mathematical results. The
planetary predictions---Saturn's hexagon from the Rossby bridge,
Jupiter's octagon from the central-vortex threshold---are
\textbf{(Derived)}, depending on measured planetary parameters.
